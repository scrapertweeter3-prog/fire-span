Why order beats average

The deck is the market. Both allocations average a positive year, but the cards arrive shuffled, and the same set of returns in a different order produces a different retirement. Two big down years in the first five, with withdrawals coming out the whole time, can end a plan that would have sailed through the identical years in a different sequence.

The 4% withdrawal figure traces to William Bengen's 1994 study of 30-year retirements, and the 1998 Trinity study found 4 to 5 percent survived most 30-year stretches of US stock and bond history. Both are averages across history, not promises for your order: Bengen's study, the Trinity study.

Fire Span is the playable version of the sequence-risk math behind Firenomics: withdrawal rates are survival questions, not round numbers.
